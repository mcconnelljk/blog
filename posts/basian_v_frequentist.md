# A Bayesian and a Frequentist Walk Into A Bar
## The Philosophical Basis of Statistical Misuse

Over the past decade, researchers have increasing focused on the challenges arising from the misuse of statistical methods across diverse applications. A thorough examination of the widespread misapplication of statistics in various academic disciplines provides valuable insights for data analysts. By examining these recurring hurdles, data analysts not only refine their analytic skills through precise and attentive application of statistical techniques to a wide range of problems, but also foster greater discernment among non-technical audiences of potentially misinterpreted findings.  Given the proliferation of advanced statistical technologies, enhanced understanding of statistical misuse is crutial for mitigating the harms arising from misapplied methods and misinterpreted results.

### The Problem of Statistics?

Across applications of surgical research, psychotherapy research, and even litigation, the elementary application of statistical techniques falls prey to a ubiquitous set of misinterpretations. In their 1994 study, *Misuse of Statistical Test in Three Decades of Psychotherapy Research* (1994), researchers Dan, Serlin & Omarmuch argue that much of the current use of statistical tests is flawed. Several researchers attribute this effect of misuse to a deficiency in statistical literacy [2]. Statistical methods, which are essentially semi-automated algorithms, are highly error-prone without the involvement of sophisticated human expertise. Additionally, a critical issue inherent in traditional statistical techniques is that they fail to clearly distinguish between causal and the associative inferences; this ambiguity can be difficult for decision-makers to navigate [2, 3].

The issue is, in part, philosophical. Over the past several decades, the shift toward pragmatic, applied research has driven an increased demand for clinically meaningful findings [2]. At the same time, academia’s tendency to treat methodologies as overarching philosophies—rather than as tools or technologies—exacerbates debates regarding the role of formal methods [3]. The decline in theoretical guidance, coupled with advances in statistical computation, has led to a rise in the use of exploratory procedures. *Misuse of Statistics in Surgical Literature* (2016) highlights the growing prevalence of advanced multivariate techniques in clinical research. Despite these advancements, a significant portion of research continues to misuse or inadequately apply statistical testing methodologies, resulting in findings that lack interpretability [6].

The widespread misuse of statistical models results in oversimplified analyses, which in turn leads to the misinterpretation of data [6]. The global reliance on hypothesis testing and the rigidly incorrect interpretation of the p-value as a definitive indicator of a position’s significance (or insignificance) reflect a pervasive misunderstanding of the nuances of statistical inference across disciplines. Moreover, the increasing use of advanced statistical techniques exacerbates the already prevalent issue of model interpretability, while also fostering a false sense of confidence [2]. As Sander Greenland aptly noted, treating models as “black boxes” that simply synthesize input into formal inferences promotes dangerous overconfidence in the results [3].

### A Battle of Frameworks

One of the most significant issues that should capture the attention of data analysts is the lack of unity within the field of statistics. The discipline is divided into competing approaches, with key distinctions between Frequentist and Bayesian methods, and further disagreements within the Frequentist camp, notably between the Fischer and Neyman-Pearson schools of statistical testing. This complex history is often overlooked in introductory statistics training, yet its implications continue to shape the misapplication of statistical methods. Although statistics is often taught as a unified framework, it more accurately resembles a phylogenetic tree of diverse approaches, each of which may be applied as appropriate depending on the data at hand. Understanding these competing frameworks empowers researchers to move beyond the long-standing misapplication and misinterpretation of a single methodology, enabling them to pursue more meaningful insights.

### Bayesian vs. Frequentist

In *An Essay Towards Solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances* (1763), published posthumously, Reverend Thomas Bayes proposed a solution to the problem of computing probability distributions by considering existing degrees of belief [7]. The resulting Bayesian approach defines probability as the measure of a person's degree of belief in an event, using this knowledge as a priori subjective probabilities in conjunction with real-world observations to determine the likelihood of an event. In contrast, the Frequentist approach to statistical inference emerged from the philosophical discourse between Fisher, Neyman, and Pearson in the early twentieth century [7]. Rather than considering real-world events and prior knowledge of their likelihood, Frequentists define probability as the ratio of the relative frequency of an event in an infinite series of trials. A key distinction between Bayesian and Frequentist approaches is that, in the Bayesian framework, the data are fixed while the parameters are adjusted to reflect a state of knowledge, whereas in the Frequentist framework, the parameters are fixed and the data are repeatedly sampled.

Among the statistical misapplications observed across various research fields, many stem from the use of frequency-based models to address problems better suited to Bayesian approaches. The dominance of the Frequentist framework in scientific research and litigation reinforces the misuse and misinterpretation of statistical models—one size does not fit all. In both litigation and scientific research, researchers are often confronted with a fixed set of 'real-world' data, against which the likelihood of a particular event must be assessed. The issue of interpreting the probability of a hypothesized event arises from the Frequentist practice of conducting repeated experiments on the same sample set when a larger population is unavailable [4]. For a Bayesian, however, the data itself serves as evidence to support a particular hypothesis [1].

### Fischer  vs  Neyman-Pearson

The Frequentist theory of hypothesis testing originated with Student's discovery of the t-distribution in 1908 [5]. Following this, R. A. Fisher, a geneticist and statistician, recognized the potential of applying “significance testing” to statistical inference in scientific research. In *Statistical Methods for Research Workers* (1925), Fisher introduced the concept of the p-value and advocated for a 5% threshold for accepting hypothesized parameters—a standard that remains widely used in research statistics today. When results are deemed “non-significant,” the Fisherian approach simply recommends collecting more data and conducting additional tests. Meanwhile, J. Neyman and E. S. Pearson advanced the concept of “hypothesis testing” as a decision-making process for evaluating and eliminating one of two alternatives. Unlike Fisher, Neyman and Pearson focused not only on the implications of accepting or rejecting the null hypothesis, but also on the risks of falsely accepting or rejecting it. Whereas Fisher’s approach to probability is rooted in inductive inference, the Neyman-Pearson framework is deductive, aiming to minimize the probability of erroneous conclusions.

The concept of hypothesis testing and the interpretation of the p-value lie at the core of many statistical misinterpretations. In striving for a more intuitive understanding of probability, the Neyman-Pearson approach oversimplifies both the problem at hand and the meaning of the p-value. In an attempt to achieve greater simplicity, research statistics have hybridized the distinct philosophies of significance testing and hypothesis testing into the practice of “null-hypothesis significance testing,” a tradition that, while dominant, is ultimately flawed [3]. Furthermore, by ignoring the original context of Fisher’s 5% rejection threshold as a personal preference, research statistics have misinterpreted “statistical significance” as a test of the truth of a proposition, rather than a measure of the model’s fit to the data. This widespread misunderstanding of significance testing frequently leads to the erroneous assumption of causality—that one of the hypotheses is true [4]. As Dar et al. note, “we can only estimate the probability of obtaining the data given the truth of the null hypothesis, not the probability of the truth of the null hypothesis given the data” [2].

### Discussion

Unlike Bayesian probability, which seeks to determine the probability of an event given incomplete knowledge—an inherently non-intuitive exercise—Frequentist probability appears more direct, though its interpretation is often falsely intuitive. Much of the misinterpretation that pervades current statistical inference stems from the attempt to ascribe real-world causality to the outcomes of an idealized, frequency-based test that assumes no prior knowledge and instead seeks to approximate a Platonic ideal of truth. Data analysts cannot afford to overlook the philosophical differences between these two mathematical approaches. Of the problems that justify the application of Frequentist methods, only a few, very specific situations warrant the use of Neyman-Pearson hypothesis testing. Unfortunately, the widespread misapplication of the hybridized Fisher/Neyman-Pearson approaches as the sole approach is encouraged by several factors.

The most significant of these factors is the pressure from research journals, which both respond to and perpetuate the tendency to treat significance levels as a graduated scale for determining causal relevance. It is fundamentally incorrect to interpret a p-value as an indicator that a hypothesis has “more” or “less” significance. Given that research failing to show significance is often not published [4], researchers tend to cite “marginally significant” or “borderline significant” results [2]. Moreover, there is no objective justification for believing that a proposition is true simply because it has reached statistical significance at Fisher’s arbitrary 5% threshold [4]. The overemphasis on this threshold by research journals places substantial pressure on researchers to misapply both hypothesis and significance tests in their pursuit of publication [3, 4].

What is often overlooked by both researchers and publishers is that, by rigorously controlling for Type II error—the error of failing to reject a false null hypothesis—the Neyman-Pearson approach shifts the burden of proof to the alternative hypothesis. The hybrid-Frequentist approach, in contrast, perpetuates a bias toward null conclusions, often mistakenly regarded as a hallmark of the scientific method [3]. Furthermore, the size of the dataset plays a crucial role in interpreting significance levels. Smaller datasets must demonstrate more extreme differences between hypothesized alternatives, while large datasets are more sensitive to even subtle differences [4]. Repeated testing of datasets also increases sensitivity to “noise.” As Dar et al. observed, there has been a proliferation of falsely rejected null hypotheses in research [2]. In relying so heavily on Fisher’s 5% significance threshold for scientific inference while also applying Neyman-Pearson’s decision-oriented approach, researchers are, in effect, increasing Type I error and incorrectly rejecting the true null hypothesis.

Finally, data analysts must consider the human preference for simplicity, clarity, and causality, which often drives the misuse and misinterpretation of statistical inference. Greenland highlights many of these incentives in *For and Against Methodologies: Some Perspectives on Recent Causal and Statistical Inference Debates* (2017). On one hand, psycho-social pressures push researchers to draw clear, unambiguous conclusions in an effort to appear significant and advance their careers. On the other hand, overconfidence in informal results may stem from a desire to justify one's labor. Moreover, researchers, publishers, jurors, judges, and business managers alike share a pervasive human aversion to uncertainty. How else can we explain the historical fascination with determining probability in mathematics?
