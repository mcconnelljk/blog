# A Bayesian and a Frequentist Walk Into A Bar
## The Philosophical Basis of Statistical Misuse

Over the past decade, researchers have increasing focused on the challenges posed by the misuse of statistical methods across various applications. A thorough examination of the widespread misapplication of statistics provides valuable insights for data analysts. By examining these challenges, data analysts not only refine their analytic skills through precise and judicial application of statistical techniques to diverse problems but also foster greater discernment among non-technical audiences of potentially misinterpreted findings. Given the proliferation of advanced statistical technologies, enhanced understanding of statistical misuse is crutial for mitigating the harms that arise from misapplied methods and misinterpreted results.

### The Problem of Statistics?

Across applications of surgical research, psychotherapy research, and even litigation, the elementary application of statistical techniques falls prey to a ubiquitous set of misinterpretations. In their 1994 study, *Misuse of Statistical Test in Three Decades of Psychotherapy Research* (1994), researchers Dan, Serlin, and Omarmuch argue that much of the current use of statistical tests is flawed. Several researchers attribute this effect of misuse to a deficiency in statistical literacy [2]. Statistical methods, which are essentially semi-automated algorithms, are highly error-prone when lacking the active involvement of human expertise. Additionally, a critical issue inherent in traditional statistical techniques is their failure to clearly distinguish between causal and associative inferences, creating ambiguity that can be challenging for decision-makers to navigate [2, 3].

The issue of misuse is, in part, philosophical. Over the past several decades, the shift toward pragmatic, applied research has increased the demand upon researchers for clinically meaningful findings [2]. At the same time, academia’s tendency to treat methodologies as overarching philosophies—rather than as tools or technologies—has intensified debates regarding the role of formal methods [3]. The decline in theoretical guidance, coupled with computational advances in statistical methods, has led to a rise in the use of exploratory procedures. *Misuse of Statistics in Surgical Literature* (2016) highlights the growing prevalence of advanced multivariate techniques in clinical research. Despite these advancements, a significant portion of research continues to misuse or inadequately apply statistical testing methodologies, resulting in findings that lack interpretability [6].

The widespread misuse of statistical models leads to oversimplified analyses, which in turn result in the misinterpretation of data [6]. The predominant reliance on hypothesis testing and the incorrect interpretation of the p-value as a definitive indicator of a position’s significance (or insignificance) reflect a pervasive misunderstanding of the nuances of statistical inference across various disciplines. Moreover, the increasing use of advanced statistical techniques exacerbates the already prevalent issue of model interpretability, while also fostering a false sense of confidence [2]. As Sander Greenland aptly noted, treating models as “black boxes” that simply synthesize input into formal inferences promotes dangerous overconfidence in the results [3].

### A Battle of Frameworks

One of the most pressing issues that should engage the attention of data analysts is the lack of unity within the field of statistics. The discipline is divided into competing approaches, with notable distinctions between Frequentist and Bayesian methods. Further disagreements extend within the Frequentist camp, notably between the Fischer and Neyman-Pearson schools of statistical testing. This complex history is often receives insufficient attention in introductory statistics training, yet it undeniably influences the application of statistical methods. While statistics is often taught as a unified framework, it more accurately resembles a diverse, phylogenetic tree, each branch of which may be an appropriate approach depending on the problem in question. Understanding these competing frameworks enables analysts to move beyond the entrenched applications and interpretations of a dominant methodology, allowing them full expression to select the approach best suited to the research question.

### Bayesian vs. Frequentist

In *An Essay Towards Solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances* (1763), published posthumously, Reverend Thomas Bayes introduced a statistical method for calculating probability distributions based on "degrees of belief"—that is, an individual’s subjective assessment of the likelihood of an event occurring [7]. The resulting **Bayesian approach** defines probability as a measure of this subjective judgment, where personal beliefs, informed by prior knowledge, are updated in conjunction with real-world observations to refine *a priori* probabilities and estimate the likelihood of an event. In contrast, the **Frequentist approach** to statistical inference, which emerged through the philosophical discussions of Fisher, Neyman, and Pearson in the early twentieth century [7], adopts a different perspective. Frequentists define probability as the ratio of the relative frequency of an event over an infinite series of trials, *excluding* both prior knowledge and subjective judgment. A key distinction between the two approaches is that, in the Bayesian framework, the data are fixed only while the parameters are adjusted to reflect evolving knowledge, whereas in the Frequentist framework, the parameters remain fixed and data are repeatedly sampled.

Among the statistical misapplications observed across various research fields, many stem from the use of frequency-based models to address problems better suited to Bayesian approaches. The dominance of the Frequentist framework in scientific research and litigation reinforces the misuse and misinterpretation of statistical models—one size does not fit all. In both litigation and scientific research, researchers are often confronted with a fixed set of 'real-world' data, against which the likelihood of a particular event must be assessed. The issue of interpreting the probability of a hypothesized event arises from the Frequentist practice of conducting repeated experiments on the same sample set when a larger population is unavailable [4]. For a Bayesian, however, the data themselves are used to update and refine the probability of a hypothesis, integrating prior knowledge with new evidence as opposed to repeated experimentation [1].

### Fischer  vs  Neyman-Pearson

The Frequentist theory of hypothesis testing originated with the discovery of the t-distribution by William Sealy Gosset, writing under the pseudonym "Student," in 1908 [5]. Subsequently, R. A. Fisher, a geneticist and statistician, recognized the utility of applying "significance testing" to statistical inference within the context of scientific research. In his seminal work, Statistical Methods for Research Workers (1925), Fisher introduced the concept of the p-value and advocated for a 5% significance threshold for accepting hypothesized parameters—a standard that continues to be widely adopted in contemporary research. When results are deemed "non-significant," the Fisherian approach typically recommends the collection of additional data and the continuation of further testing. In contrast, J. Neyman and E. S. Pearson advanced the formalization of "hypothesis testing" as a structured decision-making process to assess and eliminate one of two competing hypotheses. Unlike Fisher, who concentrated on the interpretation of evidence, Neyman and Pearson emphasized the risks associated with incorrect decisions—specifically, the dangers of falsely accepting or rejecting the null hypothesis. While Fisher’s approach to probability is rooted in inductive inference, the Neyman-Pearson framework is deductive, aiming to minimize the probability of erroneous conclusions.

The concept of hypothesis testing and the interpretation of the p-value lie at the core of many statistical misinterpretations. In striving for a more intuitive understanding of probability, the Neyman-Pearson approach tends to oversimplify both the underlying problem and the true meaning of the p-value. In an attempt to achieve greater simplicity, research statistics have hybridized the distinct philosophies of significance testing and of hypothesis testing into the dominate, albeit flawed, practice of “null-hypothesis significance testing” [3]. Furthermore, by ignoring the original context of Fisher’s 5% rejection threshold as his subjective preference, research statistics continually interpret “statistical significance” as a test of the truth of a hypothesis rather than a measure of the model’s fit to the data. This widespread misunderstanding of significance testing frequently leads to the erroneous assumption of causality [4]. As Dar et al. note, “we can only estimate the probability of obtaining the data given the truth of the null hypothesis, not the probability of the truth of the null hypothesis given the data” [2].

### Discussion

Unlike Bayesian probability, which aims to determine the probability of an event  based on incomplete knowledge—an exercise that can be counterintuitive—Frequentist probability presents a seemingly more straightforward approach, though its interpretation is often misleadingly intuitive. Much of the misinterpretation that pervades contepmoary statistical inference stems from the attempt to ascribe real-world causality to the outcomes of an idealized, frequency-based test, which assumes no prior knowledge and strives to approximate a Platonic notion of "truth". Data analysts cannot afford to overlook the philosophical differences between these two mathematical frameworks. Among the problems that justify the application of Frequentist methods, only limited set of specific circumstances warrants the use of Neyman-Pearson hypothesis testing. Regrettably, the widespread application of the hybridized Fisher/Neyman-Pearson approach as the predominant approach is reinforced by several factors.

Most significant among these factors is the pressure from research journals, which both reflect and perpetuate the tendency to treat significance levels as a graduated scale for determining causal relevance. It is fundamentally incorrect to interpret a p-value as an indicator that a hypothesis possesses “more” or “less” significance. Given the tendency for research that fails to demonstrate significance to remain unblished [4], researchers tend instead to cite results as “marginally significant” or “borderline significant” results [2]. Moreover, there is no objective basis for assuming that a proposition is true simply because it has reached statistical significance at Fisher's arbitrary 5% threshold [4]. The overemphasis that journals place on this threshold exerts substantial pressure on researchers, leading them to misapply both hypothesis testing and significance testing in their endeavor to 'publish or die' [3, 4].

Both researchers and publishers frequently overlook the fact that, , by rigorously controlling for Type II error—the failure to reject a false null hypothesis—the Neyman-Pearson approach shifts the burden of proof to the alternative hypothesis. The hybrid-Frequentist approach, in contrast, perpetuates a bias toward null conclusions, which are often mistakenly considered a hallmark of the scientific method [3]. Furthermore, the size of the dataset plays a crucial role in interpreting significance levels. Smaller datasets must exhibit more extreme differences between hypothesized alternatives, whereas largeer datasets are more sensitive to even subtle differences [4]. Repeated testing of datasets also heightens sensitivity to “noise.” As Dar et al. observed, the proliferation of falsely rejected null hypotheses is a growing issue in research [2]. By relying so heavily on Fisher’s 5% significance threshold for scientific inference while also employing Neyman-Pearson’s decision-oriented approach, researchers inadvertently increase Type I error and incorrectly reject the true null hypothesis.

Finally, data analysts must consider the human preference for simplicity, clarity, and causality, which often contributes to the misuse and misinterpretation of statistical inference. Greenland highlights many of these incentives in *For and Against Methodologies: Some Perspectives on Recent Causal and Statistical Inference Debates* (2017). On one hand, psycho-social pressures compel researchers to draw clear, unambiguous conclusions in an effort to appear significant and advance their careers. On the other hand, overconfidence in informal results may arise from a desire to justify one's invested efforts. Moreover, researchers, publishers, jurors, judges, and business managers share a deep-seated human aversion to uncertainty. How else can we account for the centuries-long fascination with probability in mathematics, if not as an enduring endeavor to understand and mitigate the randomness that shapes human decision-making?
